When You Actually Need a Chief of Staff

For founders, CEOs, and the leadership teams they run.

Everything in the workbook was written for the person becoming a Chief of Staff. This page is written for the person who might need to hire one, because more founders end up here by way of an EA who shared it with them than you'd expect.

Here's the honest answer on timing, and it's the same answer I give founders directly. Most founders I work with do not have a time problem. They have a decisions problem. You don't need a Chief of Staff because the title sounds strategic. You need one when coordination itself has quietly become the most expensive thing you do.

ORGANIZATIONAL SIGNALS
  • Your leadership meetings produce agreement in the room and no consistent follow-through afterward.
  • Initiatives nobody disputes keep slipping anyway, because nobody owns making them happen end to end.
  • You're somewhere between fifteen and a hundred people, past the stage where everyone just figures it out informally, and not yet at the size where every function has its own operations lead.
  • You're entering a new market, closing a partnership, or coordinating a joint venture, and it's eating weeks nobody budgeted for.
PERSONAL SIGNALS
  • Look at your own calendar honestly. If most of it is coordination, follow-up, and putting out fires rather than the handful of decisions that move the business, that's the tell.
  • If you're the only person who can unblock a stalled decision between two departments, you've become the company's single point of failure, and that doesn't scale.
IF YOU'RE AN EA READING THIS SECTION

Every signal above cuts both ways. If you recognized your own week in it, that's proof your organization could use a Chief of Staff, and evidence you might already be operating as one. That's exactly the case the workbook's title-conversation chapter asks you to build. Get the full workbook, read that chapter with this section in mind, then start writing it down.

Fractional or full time: which one you need

A full time Chief of Staff makes sense once the role has enough surface area to justify a dedicated person, usually once the CEO's office is a genuine 40 hour coordination surface: past 100 people, multiple geographies, heavy board cadence. A fractional Chief of Staff makes sense earlier, when the strategic load is real but sits under roughly 25 hours a week, typically somewhere in the 15 to 100 person range, or for a defined push: a fundraise, a market entry, a six to nine month transformation.

I currently work both ends of that spectrum myself: as an embedded Chief of Staff at Pioneer Outsourcing BPO, and as fractional Chief of Staff to Epena Law, a pan-African corporate law firm, alongside past work as Country Lead and Executive Partner to the Global CPO at a fintech company in Stockholm. The commitment level changes. The judgment being hired doesn't.

What to ask for, whichever model you choose

WHAT TO ASK FOR
  • 1The decision log. Every significant decision, with context and an owner. Kills re-litigation.
  • 2The initiative tracker. Every cross-functional project, current status, blockers visible.
  • 3The leadership cadence doc. Meeting rhythm, agenda standards, capture rules.
  • 4The decision-rights doc. What this person decides outright, drafts for you, and escalates, agreed in writing in week one.

If someone in this role, fractional or full time, can't produce these within two weeks of starting, you have an expensive meeting attendee, not an operating system.

FROM THE WORKBOOK — QUICK GUT CHECK
Look honestly at your calendar from last week. Which is the bigger share?
Mostly decisions only you can make
Mostly coordination, follow-up, and fires that started somewhere else
If it's the second one, the useful question isn't “do I need a Chief of Staff.” It's “what would I hand off first, and to what kind of person, fractional or full time.”
